Beam Deliberation Budget

How much time a beam predictor is allowed to spend thinking, and what follows once you write that cost into the objective.

formulated and verified 2026-09-15 · five propositions, one negative result · every claim checked against an exact dynamic program or brute force · latency figures from the Phase 1 atlas, conservative charge, pinned P-core, whole decision traced

The objective

One coherence interval of length T. A codebook of M beams. An agent spends that interval on three things and only three: probing beams at tp each, running its own predictor, and transmitting. Committing at elapsed time d to a beam of SNR γ earns the net rate

R(d, γ) = max(0, 1 − d/T) · log₂(1 + γ)

The published objective is this same expression with d counting probe time alone. Writing the predictor's own latency into d is the entire modelling change, and everything below is a consequence of it.

A strategy is then just a pair: what it costs in time, and what it delivers. Write c for its total overhead and η = G/Gmax for the spectral efficiency it achieves relative to an oracle that always picks the true best beam. On the range where it has any rate at all,

R(T) = (1 − c/T) · η · Gmax   ∝   g(T) = η · (T − c)  — a line in T.

That is the structural fact the rest of this page exploits. Comparing beam-management methods across coherence times is comparing lines, and the answers come from their upper envelope.

1 · The budget

Proposition 1 — compute budget verified: closed form is exactly the feasibility boundary at η ∈ {0.5, 0.8, 0.95, 0.999}, K ∈ {4, 8, 16}

A method that probes K beams and spends τ on computation beats the exhaustive sweep at some coherence time if and only if

τ < τ* = (M − K)·tp + csel

The accuracy η does not appear. A predictor may spend on deliberation at most the probe time it saves — no more — and being better does not buy it a larger allowance.

Proof. Both sides are (1 − d/T)+ times their efficiency, with ηS = 1. If dL < dS, then on dL < T ≤ dS the learned method has positive rate and the sweep has none. If dL ≥ dS, then for T ≤ dL the learned rate is 0 ≤ RS, and for T > dL we have η(1 − dL/T) ≤ 1·(1 − dS/T) because η ≤ 1 and dL ≥ dS. So the set where it wins is nonempty iff dL < dS, which is the displayed inequality. ∎

Assumption, and where it can fail. The “only if” direction uses ηL ≤ ηS = 1 — the sweep measures every beam and is therefore the accuracy ceiling. That is exact for noiseless probing. Under pilot noise the sweep selects argmax|zj|² and is not the oracle, so a predictor exploiting channel structure could in principle exceed it; a slower method would then still win as T → ∞, where only accuracy matters. With the specified 1,000-symbol processing gain the sweep's selection error is small and ηS ≈ 1, but τ* is a budget for methods that do not out-predict an exhaustive measurement, and should be quoted that way.

2 · The window

Proposition 2 — operating window verified against brute-force evaluation on 400,000 coherence times, agreement within 2 μs

If dL < dS and η < 1, the set of coherence times on which the learned method strictly beats the sweep is the open interval

( dL ,   Tup )   with   Tup = (dS − η·dL) / (1 − η)

Below the lower edge the method cannot finish deliberating. Above the upper edge the sweep's full gain wins, because overhead has stopped mattering. The published accounting places that lower edge at K·tp. The true edge is K·tp + τ.

So the field's standing claim — that learning wins at short coherence times — is true, but it is a claim about an interval with two edges, and the literature has only ever drawn one of them. The correction is small for light predictors and fatal for heavy ones.

3 · Which architectures are ever worth running

Proposition 3 — envelope verified against brute force at 20,000 coherence times on a log grid

Given a menu of candidate strategies {(ci, ηi)}, strategy i is optimal at some coherence time if and only if the point (ηi, −ηici) is a vertex of the upper convex hull of those points. Each surviving strategy is optimal on an interval; the intervals are ordered by increasing η, and the optimum passes from i to j at

Ti→j = (ηjcj − ηici) / (ηj − ηi)

Proof. On T > c, ordering by rate is ordering by the line g(T) = η(T − c), of slope η and intercept −ηc. The pointwise maximum of a finite family of lines is their upper envelope, whose active pieces are exactly the upper-hull vertices in (slope, intercept), in increasing order of slope. ∎

Corollary. The optimal compute depth is non-decreasing in T: deeper exits are steeper lines, and steeper lines win later. Equivalently it is non-increasing in ρ = τ/T.

This is a practical instrument, not just a picture. A menu of candidate architectures reduces to its hull vertices; everything strictly inside is dominated at every deadline and never needs to be trained, tuned, or reported. 4 · The instrument: required accuracy

Rather than assume each method's accuracy and ask who wins, fix the coherence time and ask what accuracy a method would need in order to beat a full sweep. Solving η(1 − dL/T) > (1 − dS/T) gives

5 · The adaptive schedule

Propositions 1–3 compare fixed strategies. The scheduler the plan proposes is adaptive: at each epoch it may probe, compute further, or commit. To characterise its optimum I solved the problem exactly by backward induction over states (i, k, m) — compute level, probes taken, best gain measured — which is possible precisely because the frozen latency profile makes elapsed time a deterministic function of (i, k).

Theorem 4 — structure of the optimal schedule verified on the exact DP, all @@NSLICES@@ state slices

(a) Threshold. At every (i, k) the optimal rule is: commit iff the best measured gain is at least a threshold m̄(i, k). Every state slice has a single crossing.

(b) Monotone in elapsed time. m̄ falls as time is consumed — from @@THRHI@@ at the first decision to @@THRLO@@ after 60 probes. Once committing is optimal, no amount of further elapsed time reverses it.

(c) Monotone in deliberation. m̄ rises with compute level: a better predictor makes the agent pickier, because its next probe is worth more.

(d) Separation. The schedule does not interleave. Over the range where the optimum uses any deliberation at all — per-exit costs from @@SEPLO@@ to @@SEPHI@@ μs, a @@SCALERANGE@@× span — the policy fixes a compute depth first and then probes, never returning to deliberate. The chosen depth falls as computation gets dearer, exactly as the Corollary to Proposition 3 predicts, and past the top of that range the optimum stops computing entirely, so there is nothing left to interleave and the test says nothing.

(d) is worth dwelling on, because it simplifies the proposed algorithm rather than complicating it. The three-action-per-epoch formulation is more general than the optimum needs: deliberation is chosen once, up front, and the remaining problem is ordinary optimal stopping against a shrinking threshold. On the calibrated instance the policy commits after @@MEANPROBES@@ probes on average and @@P95PROBES@@ at the 95th percentile — out of 64 available.

The scope of (d) is real and should be stated: here deliberation improves all future probes but does not condition on the measurements already taken. A predictor that re-runs on the measured buffer — which is exactly what the retired S1 stage networks did — could plausibly restore interleaving. That case is open.

6 · What does not work

Negative result — the myopic index rule is not optimal measured against the exact DP at seven coherence times

The natural one-step rule is to continue with action a exactly when the information it buys outweighs the time it costs:

ΔGa / E[Ga]  >  ca / (T − d)      relative value of information > cost as a share of remaining time

Both sides are dimensionless, the right side rises monotonically as the deadline approaches, and the rule is exactly the value-of-computation test that metareasoning would prescribe. It is also wrong. Evaluated exactly, it loses between @@OSLAMIN@@% and @@OSLAMAX@@% of the optimal value, and it is worst where this work cares most — at short coherence times.

The failure is structural, not numerical: a single probe's marginal contribution to a running maximum is small, while the cumulative contribution of many probes is large, so the myopic test stops far too early. The problem is not in the monotone case, and one-stage lookahead therefore carries no optimality guarantee here.

@@OSLA@@

This matters for how the paper positions itself. Importing the value-of-computation rule from metareasoning and calling it the algorithm would have been the obvious move, and it would have cost a fifth of the achievable rate. The contribution has to be the exact schedule, not the index.

7 · Two properties that make this usable

Proposition 5 — ρ-sufficiency verified: policy and value identical to machine precision at α ∈ {0.25, 3, 40}

Scaling every cost and the coherence time by a common factor leaves the optimal policy and the normalised value unchanged. The problem depends on hardware only through the dimensionless ratios ρ = c/T.

So the conclusions here are not pinned to an i9 and an RTX 8000. A device ten times slower reaches the identical policy at a coherence time ten times longer, and the atlas's ρ table is the portable form of its microseconds.

Proposition 6 — the controller is free measured under the Phase 1 protocol: 1,000 warmup, 10,000 timed, nearest-rank p95

By Theorem 4(a) the optimal policy is a threshold table. Computed offline it occupies @@TABLEBYTES@@ bytes, and a runtime decision is one array index and one comparison:

threshold lookup @@LOOKUP@@ μs  ·  neural stage decision @@NEURAL@@ μs  ·  @@RATIO@@× cheaper

And the gap compounds, because an adaptive controller runs at every stage, not once per interval. Over eight probe decisions the table costs @@PERINTLOOK@@ μs against @@PERINTNEURAL@@ μs for the neural rule — the difference between a rounding error and a fifth of a 1 ms coherence interval spent deciding rather than measuring.

This closes a hole that would otherwise be fatal. A controller whose job is to account for deliberation cost, but which is itself expensive to run, is self-defeating.

8 · What this establishes, and what it does not

Established

  • Propositions 1, 2, 3 and 5 are proved, and each is independently checked against brute force or the exact DP. They hold for any strategy pair, not just the measured ones.
  • Theorem 4 is verified computationally on the calibrated instance, not proved in generality. (a)–(c) are robust across every parameterisation tried; (d) is the one I would most expect to break under a different deliberation model.
  • The required-accuracy table uses no assumption about predictive accuracy.

Not established

  • The DP instance is idealised. Beam SNRs i.i.d. exponential, probes noiseless, draws with replacement, deliberation that does not condition on measurements. These are stated so they can be argued with; Phase 3's DeepMIMO channels replace the first two.
  • η is still unmeasured. The envelope illustration in §3 assumes it. §4 is the assumption-free version and is the one to cite until Phase 4 supplies real numbers.
  • Single user, single interval. No multi-user scheduling, no correlation between consecutive coherence intervals, no beam tracking across time — all of which would change what deliberation is worth.
  • Graff et al. are not overturned. Their own networks cost @@GRAFFMIN@@–@@GRAFFMAX@@ μs compiled and clear Proposition 1 comfortably; their conclusion survives the correction almost intact. What fails is the heavier architecture class the field has since adopted, and the accounting that let nobody notice the difference.

9 · What Phase 3 inherits

A specification with no free parameters left except the ones Phase 3 exists to measure. The objective is written; τ* and the window are closed forms in quantities Phase 1 already measured; the optimal schedule is a threshold table that costs @@LOOKUP@@ μs to consult; and §4 states, for each candidate architecture, the exact predictive accuracy it must demonstrate on held-out sites to be worth using at all.

That last point is the handover. Phase 3 does not need to ask which architecture is best. It needs to measure η for each, and read off the table.
